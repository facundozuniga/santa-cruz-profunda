Home › Articles › What to eat in Santa Cruz: lamb, seafood, cherries and calafate

By Santa Cruz Profunda, from Puerto San Julián · Updated 2026-11-13

In Santa Cruz, food comes from three places: the steppe, the sea and the few irrigated valleys. Lamb comes from the steppe. From the Atlantic come Argentine red shrimp, cholga mussels and blue mussels. From the valleys, cherries and berries.

This isn't a cuisine of complicated dishes. It's built on produce, slow fire, salt and patience. If you travel the province beyond El Calafate, you'll come across these flavors at estancias, farms and fishing ports. This guide tells you what to eat in Santa Cruz, where in the province to try each thing and in which months to look for it.

Patagonian lamb

What makes it different

Lamb is the province's signature dish. It's raised on open range, on natural pastures. The president of the Patagonian Sheep Meatpackers' Chamber described it as “practically organic”, with no pesticides or treatments that leave residues, according to Tiempo Argentino.

Since 2014 it has held the “Cordero Patagónico” (Patagonian Lamb) Geographical Indication. It covers more than 800,000 km², from the Colorado River southward, across six provinces, and according to Tiempo Argentino it's the largest geographical indication in the world. The same outlet reported in 2021 that 40% of the country's one million lambs are from Patagonia.

The flavor changes with the land. For chef Pablo Buzzo, lambs raised near the Andes taste more herbal and coastal ones have slightly saltier meat. In Puerto Deseado, producers say something similar: the salty pastures give it more flavor, as cook José Basualdo told Bichos de Campo.

How it's cooked

The classic way is on the asador, a cross-shaped iron spit, also called “al palo” or “a la estaca”. This is the recipe published by Tiempo Argentino:

  • A whole lamb (about 8 kg) is opened up, the rib cage is flattened and it's fastened to the spit.
  • It's set beside the fire, ribs facing the embers, tilted at about 60 degrees and 50 to 80 cm from the fire.
  • It cooks for about 2 hours on that side and is then turned.
  • It's brushed with a brine of water, salt, oregano, garlic and crushed chili, and the brushing stops once a white layer appears on the meat.
  • You use the wind to your advantage without letting the flames touch the meat.

You'll also find rolled lamb, lamb served on a plate or braised. In Puerto Deseado, José Basualdo cooks it with sun-dried tomatoes, wild mushrooms and rosemary.

Where and when to try it

Restaurants in almost every town serve it, as do many tourist estancias. In Río Gallegos, the Sociedad Rural holds the Provincial Lamb Festival together with the National Sheep Meat Festival: in 2025 they were the 38th and 27th editions, on December 5 and 6, with lamb sales, a spit-roasting contest and a folk music gathering, according to Diario Nuevo Día and WOU Noticias. The 2026 date hadn't been published when this guide was written: confirm it before you travel.

Seafood and fish from the Atlantic

What's caught in Santa Cruz

According to Argentina's national Ministry of Tourism, the most abundant seafood on the Santa Cruz coast is cholga mussels, blue mussels, squid and Argentine red shrimp. Shrimp and squid are among the province's top exports.

The most important fishing towns are Puerto Deseado and Puerto San Julián. That's where you have the best chance of eating local seafood: ask at each restaurant what's fresh that day.

Seaweed and other sea products

In Puerto Deseado, José Basualdo cooks with seaweed: he dries and grinds cachiyuyo and uses it as a seasoning, and he works with salicornia, a coastal plant known as “sea asparagus”. With these ingredients he makes sea bread and sea scones, according to Bichos de Campo. In the same town, a fishing company makes surimi from hake fillet paste.

What to order

  • Shrimp, cholga and blue mussels: on the Patagonian coast, seafood is served pickled in escabeche, in stews, au gratin or in pasta sauces, according to the Ministry of Tourism.
  • King crab (centolla): it appears in stuffed pasta, like the king crab ravioli Welcome Argentina tried at a restaurant in El Calafate. If you want to know where it comes from, ask.

Lake and river trout

Santa Cruz's lakes and rivers are a sport fishing destination. In Patagonian cooking, trout is eaten wood-smoked, baked, pan-fried or grilled, usually with garlic, lemon and rosemary, according to Google Arts & Culture's story on the flavors of Patagonia. If you catch it yourself, check the current fishing regulations first.

Los Antiguos cherries

Designation of origin

Cherries from the Los Antiguos valley have had a Designation of Origin since April 2025. It was approved by Resolution 49/2025 of Argentina's national Secretariat of Agriculture and is the country's fifth designation of origin, according to Bichos de Campo. The area runs from the south shore of Lago Buenos Aires to the surroundings of the valley, where cherries have been grown since the 1970s from trees brought from Mendoza.

What they're like

The main varieties are Bing and Lapins, and in recent years late-harvest varieties such as Sweetheart, Kordia, Regina and Skeena have been added. Growers describe them as juicy, sweet and crunchy, with a tart finish and smaller than others. The wide temperature range, strong sunlight and low humidity give them that profile.

When to go

  • Blossom: late September to late October.
  • Harvest: late December to mid-February.

On the south shore of the lake there are family farms selling fresh fruit and homemade products such as jams, liqueurs, escabeches, chutneys and tarts, and at some you can pick your own; there are also strawberries, raspberries and currants, according to the newspaper Río Negro. Which farms take visitors each season and at what times: ask at Turismo Los Antiguos. The January festival has its own guide: National Cherry Festival in Los Antiguos.

Calafate and wild fruits

Calafate (Berberis microphylla) is a thorny steppe shrub. It has a yellow flower in late spring and then a small fruit, about a centimeter across, dark, sweet and slightly tart, according to Billiken and Meteored. It's eaten straight off the bush or in jams, jellies, syrups, liqueurs and alfajores. In El Calafate it also shows up in desserts like calafate semifreddo, according to Welcome Argentina. Its color comes from anthocyanins, pigments with an antioxidant effect.

The legend, which Meteored attributes to Tehuelche (Aonikenk) and Mapuche tradition, says that whoever eats calafate returns to Patagonia. Other regional fruits you'll find in homemade jams are elderberry, blackcurrant and rosehip.

Smoked foods, escabeches and picadas

In El Calafate, Welcome Argentina found boards of regional smoked foods and picadas (sharing platters) with smoked foods and escabeches at Patagonian restaurants. It's a good way to try several products at once.

Tips for eating well in Santa Cruz

  • Check ahead with estancias. If you want spit-roasted lamb at an estancia, write ahead to find out whether they're open and whether you need to book.
  • Watch the calendar. Cherries from late December to mid-February; cherry blossoms in October.
  • Buy at the source. Farm jams and liqueurs make a good gift to take home.
  • Ask where it comes from. If you want local produce, ask whether the seafood or lamb is from the area.
  • Plan for distances. There are hundreds of kilometers without restaurants between towns: bring food for the road (Ruta 3 and Ruta 40).

Frequently asked questions

What is the typical food of Santa Cruz?

Spit-roasted Patagonian lamb. After that come seafood from the coast, Los Antiguos cherries and calafate jams and sweets.

When is cherry season in Los Antiguos?

The harvest runs from late December to mid-February, and the blossom from late September to late October.

Where can I eat seafood in Santa Cruz?

In the fishing ports, especially Puerto Deseado and Puerto San Julián.

Does Patagonian lamb have an origin label?

Yes. It has had the “Cordero Patagónico” Geographical Indication since 2014.

Is it true that whoever eats calafate comes back?

It's a Patagonian legend. There's no guarantee, but the jam is worth it anyway.
