Home › Articles › Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo: how to get there and what to see in the national park

By Santa Cruz Profunda, from Puerto San Julián · Updated 2026-10-30

In the middle of the plateau of northeastern Santa Cruz, stone trunks up to 30 m long lie scattered across the steppe. They are araucarias that grew in the Jurassic, some 150 million years ago, and were buried under volcanic ash. Today they are protected by Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo National Park. The general overview is at Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo.

Getting there takes time: you leave National Route 3 and drive about 50 km of gravel on Provincial Route 49, far from any city. In return you get a volcanic landscape dominated by the Madre e Hija hill, with guanacos and rheas. In this guide we cover how to get there, what to see, when to go and what to bring.

Where it is

The park is in Deseado Department, in northeastern Santa Cruz, within the Patagonian steppe ecoregion. It belongs to the Southern Patagonia region of the National Parks Administration and covers 78,543 ha.

The nearest towns are Jaramillo and Fitz Roy, on Route 3. The park headquarters is in Jaramillo. For more lodging options, the reference cities are Caleta Olivia, Puerto Deseado and Puerto San Julián.

Getting there

The main access is via National Route 3. At km 2,074, Provincial Route 49 branches off, a compacted gravel road. According to the park's management plan, it is 39 km to the park entrance and 11 km more to the Operations Center, where the visitor area is: about 50 km in total.

Distances are from the National Parks Administration and the province. The nearest airport with scheduled flights is Comodoro Rivadavia, where the National Parks Administration suggests renting a car. If you are traveling along the coast, also see The coast along Route 3.

The gravel road

RP 49 is gravel along the entire access stretch. The province warns that heavy rain can make it hard going for low-clearance vehicles. Drive slowly and, especially after rain or snow, check road conditions with the park headquarters before you set off.

The western access

The management plan mentions a second access from the west: from RP 12, also gravel, you take RP 49, and it is 32 km to the entrance and 25 km more to the visitor area. This is the way in from Pico Truncado (195 km) or Gobernador Gregores (330 km). The recommended access is the one from Route 3: if you plan to use the western one, ask about its condition first.

A bit of history

Geologists have been studying the trunks since 1925. In 1954 the national government created the Petrified Forest Natural Monument (Decree 7,252/54), covering about 13,700 ha. In December 2012, Law 26,825 created the national park, after the National Parks Administration added neighboring land.

The area also has a human history. According to the National Parks Administration, its cultural heritage includes traces of pre-Hispanic hunter-gatherers, the arrival of the first ranchers and the 1920–1921 events known as “la Patagonia Rebelde” (Rebel Patagonia). Welcome Argentina reports that camps, workshops, burials and quarries have been found, and that fossil wood was used to make stone tools.

What to see at Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo

The araucaria trunks

This is the main attraction. The trees are Araucaria mirabilis, a relative of today's araucarias. According to the National Parks Administration, there are trunks up to three meters in diameter and more than thirty meters long, all pointing east: they are thought to have been knocked down by winds of over 300 km/h generated by explosive eruptions. Fossilized cones have also been found.

Welcome Argentina explains that many trees remained where they grew, some with their roots and the base of the trunk still upright, which is why it is called a “forest”. According to the same source, some specimens were about 1,000 years old when they were buried.

How the stone forest formed

150 million years ago the landscape was very different: the Andes did not exist as such, the sea stretched where they are now, and the climate was warmer and wetter. Volcanic eruptions covered the forest with ash and other sediments. Silica-laden water gradually replaced the organic matter and the wood turned to stone. Later, erosion and wind exposed the trunks.

The trails

The visit is on foot. The National Parks Administration lists two trails, with mandatory registration at the Bosques Petrificados Operations Center:

In 2023 the National Parks Administration repaired the Huellas del Pasado trail and added signage. The ground is covered with stone chips that are remains of the forest itself: you may not pick anything up.

Cerro Madre e Hija and Laguna Grande

Cerro Madre e Hija is what remains of a volcano that formed about 18 million years ago, according to the National Parks Administration, and it appears on the park's emblem. Together with Laguna Grande it forms an area the park considers of high scenic value and a major store of paleoenvironmental and archaeological information. Welcome Argentina describes Laguna Grande as shallow and says it appears and disappears depending on the weather.

Steppe wildlife

The National Parks Administration mentions guanacos (the park's emblem), dwarf armadillos and gray foxes, and among birds lesser rheas, long-tailed meadowlarks, the Magellanic horned owl (tucúquere, a large owl) and the Patagonian tinamou. The Patagonian opossum, a little-known small marsupial, also lives here. Welcome Argentina adds maras, culpeo foxes, skunks and pumas, as well as the elegant crested tinamou, the crested caracara and the black-chested buzzard-eagle.

Opening hours and admission

Admission is free, according to the National Parks Administration. The published opening hours are:

  • April 1 to September 30: 10 am to 5 pm.
  • October 1 to March 31: 9 am to 7 pm.

Hours and fees may change: check before you travel at Parques Nacionales – Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo: horarios y cómo llegar.

Park headquarters contact: Teófilo Martínez s/n, Jaramillo. Phone 011 3985-3800 ext. 30001 (headquarters) or 297 423-4469 (Operations Center). Email: bosquespetrificados@apn.gob.ar.

Can you stay overnight in the park?

There are no camping areas or authorized fire pits. According to the National Parks Administration, overnight stays are only allowed in motorhomes or camper trailers in the parking areas of the Operations Center and the Madre e Hija volcano, with permission from the park rangers. The park also mentions a campground with restrooms and fire pits at Estancia La Paloma, about 20 km before the park on RP 49, and authorized campgrounds in Jaramillo. To confirm they are still open, check before you travel at Parques Nacionales – Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo: alojamiento.

When to go

The National Parks Administration recommends spring and summer; the province suggests November to April, to avoid the cold and snow. The climate is cold, dry and windy, with large temperature swings:

  • Average temperature of 19 °C in summer, with highs of up to 40 °C.
  • Average of 7 °C in winter, with lows of −15 °C.
  • Up to 200 mm of rain a year, concentrated in winter, with snowfall in the coldest months.
  • Westerly winds averaging 70 km/h, with gusts of over 140 km/h.

According to Welcome Argentina, in spring, if the winter was wet, cacti with orange flowers and daisies bloom.

How many days to stay

The visit takes a full day, mainly because of the distances and the gravel stretch. With two or three days you can add Jaramillo and Fitz Roy and combine it with Caleta Olivia, Puerto Deseado or San Julián. These are our suggestions.

Travel tips

  • Fuel: the province recommends filling up in Fitz Roy if you are coming from the north (about 140 km) or in Tres Cerros if you are coming from the south (about 92 km).
  • Water and food: the park has no water or visitor services, only public restrooms. Bring drinking water and food for the whole day.
  • Warm clothes: a windbreaker and layers even in summer, because of the wind and the temperature swings.
  • Don't pick anything up: the whole ground is paleontological heritage and taking fossil wood is prohibited.
  • Trails: register at the Operations Center, follow the park rangers' instructions and stay on the trails.
  • Waste: the National Parks Administration asks you to bring a bag and carry your trash out to a bin in town.
  • Share your plans: we found no official information on cell coverage on RP 49, so tell someone your route before you head onto the gravel.
  • Without a car: the National Parks Administration says tour agencies can be hired in Caleta Olivia, and full-day excursions are also organized from Puerto Deseado. Ask at the tourist offices what trips are running this season.

To continue your trip: Jaramillo and Fitz Roy, Puerto Deseado, Caleta Olivia and Puerto San Julián.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an entrance fee for Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo?

No. According to the National Parks Administration, admission is free. Confirm it on the official website before you travel.

How do you get to Bosques Petrificados de Jaramillo?

Take National Route 3 to km 2,074 and from there Provincial Route 49, about 50 km of gravel to the Operations Center. It is 220 km from Caleta Olivia and 230 km from Puerto San Julián.

Can you get there in a regular car?

It is about 50 km of compacted gravel. The province warns that heavy rain makes it hard going for low-clearance vehicles: drive slowly and check road conditions before you set off.

What are the visiting hours?

According to the National Parks Administration, from April 1 to September 30, 10 am to 5 pm, and from October 1 to March 31, 9 am to 7 pm.

Can you camp in the park?

There are no camping areas. Overnight stays are only allowed in motorhomes or camper trailers in the parking areas of the Operations Center and the Madre e Hija volcano, with permission from the park rangers.
